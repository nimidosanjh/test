---
title: " Beyond the Elite: A Spectrum of Use"
---

The fuller picture is more complicated than a simple "elites only" answer. The imperial, bureaucratic khipu was indeed the work of trained specialists, but recent scholarship shows the technology was never confined to the state elite. Sabine Hyland's study of the Collata khipus from a Peruvian village indicates that khipus served local governance at the community level and may encode lineage or phonetic information maintained by village authorities. Ethnographic evidence reinforces this: Andean herders and rural communities continued to use accounting and ritual khipus for centuries after the Spanish conquest, well outside any imperial framework. Khipu use thus existed along a spectrum: a restricted, prestigious specialist literacy at the imperial center, alongside more modest record-keeping accessible to local non-elites. The question of class also intersects with gender. Chroniclers depict the khipukamayuq as overwhelmingly male, yet cord and textile arts were strongly associated with women, and some scholars argue that women's record-keeping roles have been underrepresented. Ultimately, the khipukamayuq's status was occupational and knowledge-based, defined by training and office, often hereditary, and carrying genuine prestige, rather than a rigid noble caste. The medium was hierarchically structured at the level of the state, but the underlying technology diffused across social levels and outlived the empire that institutionalized it.
